# **Running Journey’s End**

In this section, you’ll learn how to run a session of Journey’s End and also receive guidance for certain scenarios.

This section will be far less focused on exact rulings and mechanics, since part of being a GM means making choices on the spot\! 

## **Starting a Journey**

The Journey, aka the Campaign, is the overall story that the player characters live and fight through. Imagine it as the narrative of a movie or a novel. The Journey has a beginning and an end, and should encompass all of a character’s development within it. When starting the Journey with your players, there is a checklist that needs to be completed before you start playing:

* Determining the Setting  
* Writing the Premise  
* Character Creation  
* Session 0


### **Determining the Setting**

The Setting of the Journey is the world in which your story will take place. You can take the world from existing media, like the continent from **Frieren: Beyond Journey’s End**, or create your own world.

If you choose to create your own world, you will need to begin by determining what kind of world it is:

* **Time Period:** Is it Medieval? Renaissance? Modern?  
* **Fantasy:** How abundant are fantastical or supernatural elements? Does magic flow through everything, or is it rare?  
* **Gods and Spirits:** Are there gods? In **Frieren: Beyond Journey’s End**, there is only the Goddess of Creation, but even she isn’t a confirmed entity. Your world may have many gods that the priests pray to, or none at all.

Now that you have the base level details, the rest of the world is done through more in-depth worldbuilding. You don’t need to have every part of the world populated with rich lore, especially since the players will likely not see everything immediately. You can write details for locations, people, and objects that you know will be important to the Journey itself.

### **Writing the Premise**

The premise of the Journey is the basic information and narrative to set up the beginning of the story. Imagine it as the synopsis or “trailer” for the Journey. It should give some details of the setting, where the players may be exploring, and what levels of danger they may be facing. It is also here where the players should be made known of certain details like how roleplay focused the campaign is, how much combat they should expect to face, and how large mystery plays into the story.

### **Character Creation**

Now with the setting and the premise, the players can begin creating their characters\! They should coordinate with each other to choose classes and abilities so that the party achieves a balance (it is always recommended to have someone who can heal).

Your role during this portion is to answer the many questions your players will likely have about the world and how their characters fit in it. When they write their backstories, you may supply locations and towns they can be from, or allow them to build parts of the world themselves.

### **Session 0**

Session 0 occurs after the setting and premise have been written, and all players have created their characters. Now, gather to finalize details, ensure that everyone has set up their character correctly, and introduce each character to the group. Play doesn’t necessarily begin here, but if your players are eager to jump in, you may have a short section of gameplay to gather the party and set up their very first quest.

## **Running a Session**

A Journey is separated into Sessions, which is any arbitrary stretch of time where you and your players have gathered to play. During a session, you and the players advance the story towards a goal, both short and long term. An entire session may be dedicated to a single quest, travelling to a far-away settlement, fighting foes, or a combination of many things.

Your role is to make sure the story progresses and make sure the players have enough information to continue. If the players ever get “stuck” and don’t know where to go or what to do, that signals to you that you should give them more clues or introduce a NPC who can help them.

Most Sessions have a defined amount of time they take up in real life. As the GM, you should probably try to find a natural stopping point in the Session so that the players do not feel arbitrarily cut off from gameplay. At the same time, you should also not go on for too long to be respectful to the schedule your group had set. Good stopping points could be: After a combat, right before a combat, when the party stops to rest, when a major piece of lore is revealed, entering a new area, and setting out to travel.

More information on what you can do during a Session will be detailed in **GM Mechanics**.

### **Writing a Journey**

How to write a Journey is an extremely subjective topic. There are an infinite possibilities on how to start, continue, and end the Journey. What is important is that your player’s have some idea of the structure, not intimate details, but just the structure so that they aren’t blindsided by a genre they were not expected.

If you are writing a heroic Journey where the player characters save or massively aid the world, then the Hero’s Journey story structure is a good structure to use. Depending on the kind of story you want to tell, you can borrow story structures of existing literature.

## **Ending a Journey**

The end of a Journey is often a grand moment, bittersweet as your group bids farewell to the characters they have played as, talked to, and fought with for a long time. When your group ends a Journey, time and Focus should be given to each character to describe how their life goes on after the story. Do they settle down? Keep adventuring? How do they make a living after the excitement of the Journey?

When the curtains draw for the final time of this Journey, it is finally laid to rest and should your group decide to, you may start looking forward to the next Journey.

# **GM Mechanics**

The following sections are mechanics and rules specifically for you, the GM. These are also more specific pieces of advice on how to operate certain parts of Journey’s End during a session.

## **GM Checks and Actions**

Actions for you function exactly the same as any player character, except you will be making them for many different characters across a journey.

## **Calling for Checks**

When a player describes an actions or move they want to make during the game, assess whether or not there is a degree of difficulty to what they want to accomplish. If they are using a feature, making an attack, casting a spell, or using a technique, then it will say in the description if a check is needed.

If you determined there is difficulty to their Action, then assess exactly how difficult the Action is. This process relies on a lot of factors, but you can base the DC using the following guide:

| Difficulty Level | DC |
| :---- | :---- |
| 5 | Easy |
| 10 | Average |
| 15 | Hard |
| 20 | Very Hard |
| 25 | Nearly Impossible |

The difficulty you choose does not have to be in multiples of 5, as you can place the DC somewhere in between. Also, you, as the GM, can choose whether to reveal the DC before the players roll, after, or to keep it secret.

### 

### **Guaranteeing a Check**

Sometimes, the players really need to succeed on a check. Or, their roll is so close that it is soul-crushing to fail it. When this happens, you may offer them a choice to succeed instead of failing in exchange for one of the following:

* Spending **Stamina**  
* Spending a **Miracle**  
* Losing **Hit Points**  
* Giving you **Doom**

You could also establish other penalties for guaranteeing the check depending on the context of the current scene. Maybe they drop a fragile item they’re holding, or an enemy they’re grappling with breaks free?

### **Granting Advantage and Disadvantage**

Without the players spending a **Miracle**, using abilities, or you spending **Doom**, almost all **Checks** will be made “straight,” or without advantage or disadvantage. However, you can occasionally grant either if you deem it appropriate to the narrative.

Maybe the player is trying to make an **Agility check** through deep snow that would hinder their movement. A guard that they already tried to trick is now much less likely to believe any **Charisma checks**.

You might be wondering why not just increase the **DC** of the **check**? You certainly can, but the **DC** can often make **checks** feel ridiculously easy or difficult, whereas advantage and disadvantage give players a more tangible feeling of how the stakes change.

## **GM Actions**

As described in the **Rules of Play** section, you will often take the **Focus** from the players in order to advance the world, environment, and adversaries to the party. When you do so, it is important to know what to do with the **Focus**.

### **Choosing a GM Move**

Most of your GM moves will depend on what the players had just done before. If a check is involved, you can use their success or failure to determine how the situation unfolds.

#### Players rolled a Success

When the players rolled a success, you usually take **Focus** to describe how the players advance in their goals. Sometimes, however, you’ll spend **Doom** immediately to introduce a new complication. When that happens, you should describe how the success of the players might have forced the hand of whatever they are facing right now. Perhaps the villain was underestimating them before and is now fighting more seriously, or the corrupt noble the party is investigating begins to panic as they get closer to finding the evidence.

What is important is to make sure NOT to undermine their success. Did a player just manage to knock an enemy off their feet? Maybe have the enemy try to drag the player down with them instead of having them just stand up without any consequences. That isn’t to say you should never immediately reverse an effect, but those moments should be reserved for powerful adversaries to show how dire the situation is.

### 

#### Players rolled a Failure

When the players rolled a failure, you usually take the **Focus** to describe how the players failed to advance in their goals. Sometimes, you might also spend **Doom** to further complicate their objective.

It is important that you always keep the story moving. If they fail to lockpick a door, don’t stop at just saying that they failed\! A guard might walk out that very door to catch them\! Especially if you spend **Doom**, the story should advance to another event, just one that is unfavorable for the players.

### **Enemy Actions**

When you take the Focus in combat, you’ll almost always use it to make an enemy attack the players. All enemies have a standard attack, shown on their statblock, which can target a single player by default.

You make attacks and actions the exact same as a player would given those abilities.

## **Doom**

How you gain **Doom** and use it was already described in the **Rules of Play** section, so it won’t be reiterated here. Rather, this will be advice on how often and in what ways you should be using it.

### **Tracking Doom**

You should always keep track of how much **Doom** you have at once. You can do so by keeping coins as “tokens”, tracking the number using a digital counter, or using a d10 dice.

### **Spending Doom**

You should be spending **Doom** whenever you feel the situation calls for it. Have the players been succeeding many time in a row? Complicate the situation with **Doom.** Have the players failed at a critical moment? Show just how dire the consequences are by spending a couple **Doom**.

It is good to avoid hoarding **Doom** and then spending it all in one place. The back and forth of players utilizing their abilities and **Miracles** with your **Doom** often creates dynamic scenes. It will be much more satisfying for you and the players when their characters achieve a hard-earned victory or suffer a grave defeat.

### **How Much Doom Should I Spend?**

Like many things in this section, this is a subjective question. However, the difficulty of a scenario directly scales with the amount of **Doom** you intend to spend on it. Thus, the following is a rough guide on how much **Doom** should be spent on a given encounter:

| Type | Description | \# of Doom |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Trivial | A peaceful scene that is very unlikely to involve anything of high stakes, such as travel, shopping, or a conversation between friends. | 0-2 |
| Standard | A scene that involves tension with a notable objective, such as an ambush, investigation, or social situation | 1-3 |
| Difficult | A dire scene against a major opponent or obstacle, such as a large fight or a character-defining moment. | 4-6 |
| Deadly | A climactic confrontation with a villain of the Journey, such as a boss fight or determining the fate of a character. | 7-10 |

### **Doom Surplus**

If you have a lot of **Doom** and are having trouble figuring out what to do with it, here are some possible uses:

* Introduce a New Enemy  
* Place a New Obstacle in the Player’s Path  
* Act First in Combat  
* Trigger a Sudden Environmental Change  
* Influence the World in a Major Way

You can always improvise **Doom Actions** in order to make an encounter or combat more interesting. Maybe the floor suddenly caves in or a pillar in the dungeon crumbles onto the players’ heads.

## **Progress Counters**

A Progress Counter is a way to track progress towards events, consequences, or goals. Some enemy statblocks or encounters may explicitly call for a Progress Counter, but you may introduce them for a multitude of reasons.

### **Progress Die**

To represent a Progress Counter, you can often use a die, with a 1 being the beginning and the highest value of the die being the culmination of the Progress Counter. This way, not only can you track it, but the players can also see how far along the Progress Counter has progressed.

The exact die you use is dependent on how long you want the Progress Counter to take. However, it is recommended to only use d12s or smaller dice.

### **Advancing a Progress Counter**

You can set the exact trigger to advance a Progress Counter, though it is recommended to tell the players exactly what that trigger is. Usual triggers include:

* Players taking an **Action**  
* Players rolling a Success/Failure on a **check**  
* Players making steps to solve a puzzle  
* Players defeating an enemy  
* Players taking a rest  
* You spend **Doom**

Note how almost all of these triggers are directly related to how the players act and behave. 

## **Coin, Equipment, and Loot**

Most adventurers rely on coin and their equipment in order to survive the Journey. The players may rob coin from a corrupt noble, or the enemies they defeated may have an interesting item.

### **Distributing Coin** 

Whenever you give out coin to the players, it is usually a good idea to give an equal amount of coin to each player character, so that everyone has around equal spending power. Of course, if a player negotiates for a higher pay for themselves, or takes on a job by themselves, they then get additional coin.

At character creation, you determine how much coin players get. A good default, however, is 10 gold.

### **Distributing Equipment**

Usually, you give Equipment to players as a quest reward or for finding it in the world. Its difficult to give equal amounts of equipment to players everytime they do get Equipment.

At character creation, players choose from a list of starting equipment. If you choose so, you can also allow players to choose from a list of starting magical items.

# **General Guidance**

Here you’ll find general principles that you should follow when playing as the GM. While you shouldn’t take everything said here as law, these are principles that have led to many great stories.

## **Being the GM**

As the Game Master (GM), it is your responsibility to guide the narrative and playing as the world that your player’s exist in. You are the blizzard freezing them, the merchant they are haggling with, and the villain fighting against them. Being the GM is one of the most exhilarating and fulfilling experiences, but it can also be the most daunting task for someone.

As a new GM, it is recommended to take things slowly and understand that you are probably learning multiple skills at once. Don’t worry if you occasionally make mistakes or mess up\! Everyone starts from as new and you will improve over time.

## **Magic is the Realm of Imagination**

Remember this from the **Intro**? It also applies to how you can run the game. Journey’s End is inherently a heroic high fantasy game, but if the rules of the game gets in the way of the narrative and what your players want to achieve, then you should bend the rules to allow it. Maybe a Mage can’t **augment** their spell anymore, but this is a fight against their evil mentor. You may choose to allow the Mage to double the tier of their spells for this moment, and decide a consequence of pushing themselves later.

That does not mean you should always bend the rules. Otherwise, the rules no longer mean anything. Consistency within a game is important and exceptions to the rule should remain exceptions.

### **Rule Bending as a Mechanic**

If you actually want players to be able to bend the rules more, you can choose to use this rule.

An additional way of using a **Miracle** is to accomplish something that would otherwise be impossible. You may augment a spell more than its Tier allows, or delay making a Death Action when you are reduced to 0 Hit Points. The scope of what can be done with this rule is up to the GM.

## **Begin and End with the Narrative**

During all sections of gameplay, you should start with the narrative, describing the scene, before going to mechanics. When the mechanics are resolved, you should end by describing the consequences of the Actions taken.

## **Collaboration, Not Adversity**

At all times, Journey’s End is meant to be a collaboration between you and your players to tell a great story. Part of your job will be to play as the enemy and others who would oppose the player characters. However, it is critical to remember that the players are not your enemy. Their characters are the enemy to the villains of the story, but you as the GM are always on the sides of the players. That side being the one aiming to create a great story.

Part of the collaboration is to be a fan of the characters they play. You should be proud when they develop as a character, excited when they perform amazing feats, and sad when their journey meets its end. Your goal is never to absolutely crush the player characters or thrust them into an unwinnable situation. You should be giving them a challenge that can be overcome, though perhaps with great difficulty.

## **Let Your Players Fill Your World**

You don’t have to create the lore for every nook and cranny of the world you play in. If a player character comes from a city in your world, let them take charge to build that environment. How they grew up and what they encountered can help fill your world and make it feel alive.

## **Involve the Backstories of Your Players**

As the GM, you likely have the story you want to tell. The great villain who wants to change the world against the players’s would-be heroes. However, you should make time to face the problems and questions the player character’s have about their past. A way to do both is to connect a player character’s backstory to the main story itself. Maybe the Warrior has a missing brother, and they find clues to their fate while being captured by a villain.
